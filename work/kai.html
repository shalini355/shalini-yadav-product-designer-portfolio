Kai · Wellness · Solo

Kai

An AI mood reflection companion that speaks Hinglish — designed for students who think and feel in a language most wellness apps ignore.

Part 1 · Context & Hook

The problem nobody was naming.

Mental wellness apps in India have a language problem. Every major platform — Wysa, YourDOST, Headspace — is built in formal English. But most urban Indian students do not feel in formal English. They feel in Hinglish. When something goes wrong, the internal monologue is not "I am experiencing significant academic stress today." It is "yaar aaj bahut stressed tha, pata nahi kya ho raha hai."

That gap — between how a user actually feels and the language the product forces them to use — is not a minor UX inconvenience. It is the reason people open a wellness app once and never return. The product does not feel like it is talking to them. It feels like a form they have to fill out.

The design objective was specific: build a chat interface where a student can type in natural Hinglish and receive a response that feels warm, contextually aware, and private — not clinical, not translated, not corrected.

Part 2 · Constraints & Trade-offs

The messy reality.

Solo project. No budget. Free-tier AI API with hard rate limits. Every design decision had a cost attached — in time, in complexity, or in scope. Here is the honest version.

Free-tier API rate limits

Under concurrent sessions or rapid message sending, API calls fail silently or return a 429 error. A generic error screen was the obvious solution. It was also the wrong one — a user in a vulnerable emotional state who sees a broken app does not try again. They leave.

Decision: after 3 consecutive failed calls, Kai shifts into a rule-based mode and sends "I'm having a slow moment — let's keep it simple." The conversation continues. No 500 error. No broken state.

Anonymous-to-registered session merge

Mandatory sign-in before any chat caused hesitation in all 4 hallway testers. Two asked "why do I need an account just to talk?" Making sign-in optional introduced a technical edge case: a guest user who signs up mid-conversation has their chat history orphaned under an anonymous UID.

Decision: Firebase's linkWithCredential() merges the anonymous session into the new authenticated account, preserving all chat documents. This is invisible in Figma. It only exists if you understand the auth model.

Hinglish sentiment model accuracy

There is no production-grade off-the-shelf sentiment model for Hinglish. English-trained models score "yaar bahut bura lag raha hai" as neutral because the individual English tokens carry no negative weight.

Decision: manually labelled 60 Hinglish phrases across three sentiment categories and built a keyword-based scoring dictionary as a v1 stopgap. It fails on sarcasm and regional slang. This limitation is documented — not hidden.

Scope cut — weekly reflection report

Three days before the deadline I cut the weekly reflection report entirely. The screens were finished in Figma. The backend schema required to support it — aggregating session data by week, storing keyword frequency, generating the summary — would have doubled Firestore complexity and added at least 3 days of build time.

Lesson: design the data model before designing the screen. For any feature that displays aggregated data, the first question is not "what should this look like?" It is "what does the database need to store, and how expensive is that query?"

Part 3 · Iterations & Key Decision Points

Two layouts. One clear winner — but not for the reason I expected.

Option A — Full-screen chat

WhatsApp-style single stream

The entire screen is the chat. Mood data and analytics are accessible only through a separate tab — never visible during an active conversation. One focus. One thread.

Option B — Split-panel layout

Chat left, mood summary right

Chat on the left (60% width). A live mood summary panel on the right: current sentiment score, a trend sparkline, and keyword tags extracted from the conversation in real time.

Why Option B was discarded

I ran informal think-aloud sessions with 4 classmates using a clickable Figma prototype of both layouts. The split-panel failed for one specific reason that no amount of design theory would have predicted without testing: users felt watched.

Two out of four testers, unprompted, used the word "uncomfortable" when the mood panel updated in real time during a vulnerable message. One tester stopped typing mid-sentence, looked at the sentiment indicator, and said "wait, it's judging me right now?"

That is the opposite of the emotional register Kai is supposed to create. The mood panel — designed to feel empowering — felt surveillance-like in practice. The data was correct. The placement was wrong.

Decision: moved all mood analytics to a separate, intentionally accessed tab. Users choose when to look at their mood data. It is never passively surfaced during a conversation. This is the difference between designing for a feature and designing for a feeling.

Part 4 · Final Solution & Technical Edge

What the prototype covers.

A full-screen Hinglish-aware chat interface, a graceful AI fallback, an intentionally accessed mood analytics tab, and an optional sign-in flow — all designed with the implementation model in mind, not just the visual layer.

The chat interface

Full-screen, single-stream conversation. Hinglish input accepted without correction or translation. Kai's responses adapt tone based on keyword sentiment scoring — warmer and more check-in-oriented when distress keywords are detected, lighter when positive keywords dominate.

The fallback state

After 3 failed API calls, Kai switches to rule-based mode. The transition is disclosed in character: "I'm having a slow moment — let's keep it simple." The conversation continues with pre-written supportive responses. No error screen. No broken state.

The mood analytics tab

Accessed intentionally via bottom navigation. Shows a mood trend chart across recent sessions, a session history list, and the top emotional keywords from recent conversations. Never visible during an active chat.

The optional sign-in prompt

Appears as a card at the end of the first session. Two options: "Save my history" (leads to sign-up) and "Maybe later" (dismisses and continues as guest). No pressure, no gate. Firebase's linkWithCredential() handles the anonymous-to-registered session merge without data loss.

The CS advantage

Three things a non-technical designer would not have caught

The anonymous session merge required understanding Firebase's anonymous authentication and the linkWithCredential() merge path — invisible in Figma, critical in production. The fallback trigger logic required a retry counter, a threshold check, and a state flag that switches the response handler from AI-generated to rule-based. The sentiment scoring architecture uses a local JSON keyword dictionary — zero latency, no rate limit, works offline. The trade-off (limited accuracy) is documented. The architectural choice was deliberate.

Part 5 · Impact, Hiccups & Honest Learnings

Target metrics and what I would do differently.

This project is not live. These are the metrics that would validate or invalidate the core design decisions if it were deployed.

Session completion Target > 70%
Day-7 return rate Target > 30%
Fallback trigger rate Target < 15%
Mood tab open rate Target > 40%

The scope cut that taught me the most

I designed the weekly reflection report before I designed the data model that would support it. The UI was finished. The Figma screens looked good. Then I sat down to plan the Firestore schema and realised the aggregation logic would require a Cloud Function running on a schedule, a separate collection for weekly summaries, and significantly more read/write operations than the rest of the app combined. I cut the feature 3 days before my deadline.

The sentiment model ceiling

The 60-phrase keyword dictionary works for the phrases I labelled. It fails on sarcasm, on regional slang outside my own vocabulary, and on sentences where the emotional weight is carried by context rather than individual words. "Haan bilkul, sab theek hai" said sarcastically scores as positive. It is not positive. I documented this as a known v1 limitation rather than pretending it does not exist.

The split-panel assumption

I assumed real-time mood visibility would feel empowering. It felt like surveillance. This was the most important thing hallway testing taught me: the gap between what a feature is designed to do and what it actually makes a user feel can only be found by watching a real person use it. No amount of design rationale closes that gap.

What I would do differently

Start with a content audit of what data I can actually collect before designing any data-visualisation feature. Define the data model before designing the screen. Test the feeling, not just the flow — usability testing catches broken flows, but only emotional observation catches broken feelings.
